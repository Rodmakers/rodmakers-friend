📖 How to use Rodmaker’s Friend

Opening it

On a computer — double-click the file. It opens in your web browser. Keep it on your desktop, or bookmark the page once it's open.

On an iPhone or iPad — tapping the file in Mail or Files shows a dead page, because Apple's quick preview won't run it. Save the file to Files or iCloud Drive, then open it with any free HTML viewer app from the App Store. It then works fully, including offline.

On Android — save the file and open it with Chrome. It works directly.

Using it

1 · My Shop — start here. Check the equipment you own and add details if you like. Anything needing gear you don't have is greyed out later, so the app only offers you paths you can actually build. Some options need more than owning the tool — they need one long enough, so set the station count on your hand mill and planing forms. A full-length blank, for instance, needs a setup that reaches the whole strip; with a standard 13-station mill it stays available but the follow-up makes you commit to blending overlapping passes, and the one-pass option is greyed out with the numbers.

Two of the same thing — if you own more than one of something a chosen option runs on, a Which one picker appears under that option in My Process, and beside the settings table for the mill. Two hand mills of different lengths give different station tables, so the app has to know which mill this rod is on. With only one of a thing, nothing is asked.

A rod and a method are different things — worth getting straight once. A rod is one rod you are making: its taper, its ferrules, its numbers, its checklist and everything you have checked off on it. You pick which rod you are on in the Rod bar at the top of the page, see them all under My Rods, and My Process names the one you are working on at the top. A method is your usual way of building — a set of choices and numbers kept so you can drop it onto any rod, under Saved methods in My Process. Methods belong to your shop, not to a rod, so that list does not change when you change rods; the line beside it tells you which method, if any, the rod you are on is following.

2 · Rod basics — the top of My Process, and where a build actually starts. Pull-downs for units, rod length, line weight, pieces, tips, cross-section and solid or hollow-built, plus a free-text taper name so a rod is recorded as "Payne 101" rather than "a classic taper" — the name goes on the graph, the settings panel and every export. Every pull-down starts on — choose —: nothing is filled in for you, and the summary tiles below stay blank until you decide. On a multi-piece rod the grid also carries the ferrule: the style — Super Z, traditional step-down, Dickerson-type step-down or other — its size in 64ths, and where it sits. Only the Dickerson type steps the cane itself, so only it asks for two sizes (e.g. 13 to 12) and only it says anything about it in the taper record; on the others the step is in the metal and the cane is one size both sides. Whatever you set, the cane is planed to the ferrule at the joint and every settings table carries it — for a Dickerson, the tip section to 12/64 and the butt to 13/64. Leave the station blank and the rod is split into equal sections. The sizes are repeated on the Pieces card. Ferrule material and supplier live in the Mount Ferrules stage.

Hollow-built rods — you are never asked how the hollowing is specified; the taper record answers it. Add a third value on a line — station, flat-to-flat, wall — and that is a station-by-station hollow taper. Two lines are enough: a wall where the hollow starts and a wall where it ends, with the wall interpolated across everything between them. Outside the first and last walled line the rod is solid. A wall of 0 means solid at that station, which is how you close one hollow run and open another further along. Leave the third column off and the hollow is the range fields sitting with the settings table: from here to there, with the wall at each end. Switch between them just by adding or removing the wall column. The graph carries a dashed hollow size line below the taper, and the space between the two lines is the hollow itself. At an OD of 0.320″ with a 0.060″ wall the hollow is 0.200″, so the dashed line sits at 0.120″ and you read the cavity off the gap above it. As the cane narrows the hollow shrinks, the gap closes, and the dashed line rises until it merges into the taper at the station the walls meet — a 0.040″ wall in 0.080″ of cane. That station is solid in the table, the dashed line ends there on the taper, and the legend names it: runs out at 5.1″. It is solved to the exact station even when that falls between the ones you typed, rather than stopping short. It breaks and picks up again wherever the cane is big enough to take the wall.

What this doesn't do — stress curves and line-weight prediction. Design or check a taper in Hexrod, RodDNA, CaneCalc, DynaRod, etc., then paste or type the station dimensions into the Taper record here and this app takes over from there: form and mill settings, strip counts, ferrule sizing and the build checklist.

Ferrule stations — a ferrule station carries two dimensions. Enter it as two lines in the taper record, same station, tip dimension first, then the butt. The Ferrules panel then shows both dimensions and both sizes, and the settings tables split the rod where you actually put the joint instead of assuming equal sections. Sizes round up to the next 64th so the ferrule is never smaller than the cane, with a thousandth of slack so a dimension already on a 64th stays put. When the two sides come out at different sizes the panel names it a step-down and writes it butt-first, the way you'd order it.

Units — Imperial (inches, °F) or Metric (millimetres, °C), also unchosen at the start; imperial is shown until you pick. Switching later converts everything you've already entered — taper, oversizes, hollow range, temperatures — so no number changes meaning. One thing stays put: mill and planing-form stations are physically 5 inches apart, so the station grid doesn't move; metric just shows it as 12.7 cm.

3 · My Process — the stages of a rod build in order, starting at the culm. There is no design stage: what you would settle there is either on the Rod basics panel above or asked at the settings table where it changes a number, and the questions that used to sit in it now live in the stage where the work happens. Node strategy — staggered or nodeless — opens Node Work. How you cover a full-length strip with your tooling, and whether the hollowing is fluted or scalloped with dams, are asked in Final Planing. Open a stage, read the options, tap the one you use. A question already answered elsewhere is not asked twice, and it is not read back to you either. Nothing is pre-chosen — every decision is yours, and answering one often opens follow-up questions. Tap details on any option to read how it's done and what it changes downstream.

Planing targets — the settings table shows the geometry (rod dimension, half dimension, taper setting) as soon as there is a taper, but it will not print a strip target until you have said how you are getting there. Before that there is a rough-taper question: if you cut a rough taper the tables carry a rough target at every station — the final half dimension plus your own oversize — and if you only bevel the strips to a straight triangle there is no rough target, so no column. Both questions can be answered with the buttons on the card itself; nothing jumps you elsewhere. On the hand mill the second question is whether you bind your strips and check for gaps before planing to final, and until you answer it no target column appears. Answering yes is what makes it two sessions: to correct a gap you need material still on the strip, so you mill oversize, bind dry to find the gaps, fix them, then take the strips to final. That is why only the first session carries an oversize. The final target is the taper dimension plus the enamel if the enamel is still on, and nothing at all if it is already off — that margin is the enamel's own thickness, not a separate allowance.

Strips per culm — set by section, because a culm does not give up the same number of each. Butt strips have to come from the biggest, best cane; tips can come from more of it. The planner shows what each section needs, shares your spares across them, and works out the culms from whichever section runs out first — it names that section, so you can see whether raising your tip yield would help or whether the butts are the constraint.

4 · My Build Checklist — builds itself from your choices, and holds bench work only. Nothing settled on the Rod basics panel is restated as a step to check off, because that would only send you in a circle; the work those answers imply is filed where the work happens, so fluting lands in Final Planing and nodeless splicing lands in Node Work. Standing shop practice is out too: how you store cane, how you keep an iron sharp, how you keep dust off a curing rod. Those are how you run a shop, not steps in this rod. A stage stays locked until you've made its decisions; tap the amber prompt to jump straight to whatever's missing. Check steps off as you work, add your own steps, delete ones you don't use, and attach a note to any step with the ✎.

5 · My Rods — every rod you have built or planned on this device, one row each: number and name, taper name, length, line weight, how it is built, how far through the checklist it is, when you started it and when you last worked on it. Sort by any of those by tapping the heading, or search across names, tapers and dates. Each row reads that rod's own saved state in that rod's own units, so a metric rod and an imperial one sit in the same list without either changing. open switches to it, copy starts a new rod with the same choices and a fresh checklist, file saves that one rod out, and ✕ deletes it. 🖨 Print list gives you the whole rack on paper. There is no practical limit — a fully specified rod takes about a kilobyte, so the browser's storage holds thousands.

6 · Your numbers — times, temperatures, oversizes and dimensions appear as editable fields. Change them to your own practice; the tables and checklist follow.

Keeping your rods safe

Read this once. Your rods are held in the browser's own storage for this file. That is convenient, and it is not a backup. The rods are yours — but the storage holding them is the browser's to clear, and several ordinary things erase it: clearing browsing data or "cookies and site data", "Remove All Website Data" in Safari, resetting or reinstalling the browser, a new computer, or a private/incognito window (which never saves anything at all). A browser may also clear storage on its own if it decides the space is unused or is running short. Nothing about that is a fault in this app — it is what browser storage is.

So save your rods to a file. In My Rods, 💾 Save all rods writes every rod, every shop profile and every saved method into a single file — rodmakers-friend-rods-2026-08-14.rodlib.json or similar — and puts it in your Downloads. Move it somewhere you actually back up: iCloud Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, a Documents folder that gets backed up, a USB stick. That file is yours and is readable by any computer; the browser cannot touch it.

The banner tells you where you stand. My Rods shows a green line when every rod is in a saved file, and an amber one naming how many rods have changed since — with the save button right there. Save again whenever it goes amber; it takes a second and it overwrites nothing, because each save is a new dated file.

To recover after a wipe, a new browser or a new computer — open Rodmaker's Friend, go to My Rods, click 📂 Restore rods and choose your saved file. Every rod comes back with its taper, choices, numbers, notes and checked-off steps, along with your shop profiles and methods. Restoring merges rather than overwrites: rods that aren't there are added, rods that are there are only touched if the file's copy is newer, and anything you have done since is left alone. Restoring the same file twice changes nothing the second time, so there is no way to make it worse by trying. If you land on a fresh, empty copy of the app, the blank starter rod is cleared away for you.

Habits worth having — save all rods at the end of any session where you got real work done; keep the file in a synced folder so it is off the machine; and if a rod really matters, 💾 Save this rod to a file in the rod bar writes that single rod out on its own. To move rods to another computer, save all rods on one and restore on the other.

Saving & sharing

Everything saves automatically on this device as you go — close it and come back later. Nothing is uploaded anywhere and nothing is shared; your rods stay with you. The rods live in the browser's own storage for this file, not inside the file itself and not in any document another program can read; My Rods is how you see them all, 💾 Save all rods is how you back them up, and 💾 Save this rod to a file is how you get a single rod out.

Use 💾 Save this rod to a file to keep a backup, move a rod to another device, or send it to someone. 📂 Open a rod file loads one back.

Each device keeps its own copy, so your phone and computer won't sync by themselves — move a build between them with a build file.

When a new version of the file arrives — your saved rods, methods and shop normally carry straight over, because they live in your browser rather than inside the file. Open the new one the same way you opened the old. Two things to know: if a version adds new decisions, they'll show as undecided on existing rods (answer them and the checklist unlocks again), and if you switch browsers or viewer apps the new one starts empty. A quick 💾 Save this rod to a file before updating takes a moment and removes the question entirely.

If something looks wrong

Note the version shown at the top of the page and what you'd chosen at the time. Sending your saved build file along makes it easy to see exactly what you were looking at.
